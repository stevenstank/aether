The harbor was quiet before dawn. The fishing boats rocked against the pier,
and the gulls, who never seem to sleep, circled the masts and called to one
another. Old Marta walked the length of the pier the way she had every morning
for forty years: slowly, deliberately, counting the boats.

"Twelve," she said to no one. "Twelve, and the Silver Heron makes thirteen."

But the Silver Heron wasn't there. Its mooring line hung slack in the water,
and the iron ring it had been tied to was bright where the rope had worn it.
Marta didn't panic. She'd seen boats go out early before; she'd seen boats
come back late. She'd seen, twice, boats that didn't come back at all.

She sat on the bench by the harbor master's office and waited. The sun came
up over the breakwater. The gulls quieted. At half past six, the harbor
master himself arrived, carrying two cups of coffee, and handed one to her
without a word. They had done this, too, for a long time.

"The Heron?" he asked at last.
"Gone before I came," she said. "Before five, then."
"Before five," he agreed, and they watched the water together.
